**A learning path in order, not a pile of links.** Four stages, in the order I would take them, and what each
course gives you.

**[Start with CS50's Introduction to AI →](https://cs50.harvard.edu/ai/2024/)**

## 1. A first look

- **[CS50's Introduction to Artificial Intelligence with Python](https://cs50.harvard.edu/ai/2024/)**: the ideas
  of AI at a high level.
- **[Machine Learning Crash Course](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course)**: a short, practical
  start on machine learning.

## 2. The foundations

- **[CS188, Introduction to AI (Berkeley)](https://berkeleyai.github.io/cs188-website/)**: graded assignments and
  projects. It expects data structures and algorithms, but effort gets you through.
- **[MIT 6.036, Introduction to Machine Learning](https://openlearninglibrary.mit.edu/courses/course-v1:MITx+6.036+1T2019/course/)**:
  more mathematics, and the algorithms written from scratch.
- **[Machine Learning Specialization (Andrew Ng)](https://www.coursera.org/specializations/machine-learning-introduction)**:
  the concepts explained with a lot of patience.

## 3. Go deeper, in one direction

- **[MIT MicroMasters in Statistics and Data Science](https://micromasters.mit.edu/ds/)**: five courses over 12 to 16
  months. Start with machine learning or probability, then statistics (harder, but manageable).
- **[DeepLearning.AI](https://www.deeplearning.ai/courses/)**: start with the Deep Learning Specialization, then
  NLP, computer vision or generative AI.

## 4. Side roads worth taking

- **[CS231n, Deep Learning for Computer Vision](https://cs231n.stanford.edu/)**, with its
  [lecture videos](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL3FW7Lu3i5JvHM8ljYj-zLfQRF3EO8sYv).
- **[CS285, Deep Reinforcement Learning (Berkeley)](https://rail.eecs.berkeley.edu/deeprlcourse/)**.
- **[MIT 18.06, Linear Algebra (Gilbert Strang)](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/)**.

## After the courses

After about a year of courses you will feel ready. Then pick one problem, your own or one everyone has, and work on
it for a year or two. You will find that training the model is less than a tenth of the work. The rest is deciding
where machine learning fits, and turning the problem into one it can solve.

<details>
<summary><b>Jobs in AI and ML</b></summary>

Research roles usually ask for a PhD; companies rarely hire straight out of a bachelor's degree for research. A
master's followed by a PhD, or a direct PhD, is the usual route, and large research labs hire from there.
Engineering roles (AI developer, ML engineer, data scientist) are open at startups and large companies alike: keep
applying, and keep meeting people.
</details>

AI without mathematics is like a Lamborghini without an engine. It's cool, but it doesn't work.
